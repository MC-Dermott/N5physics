Fusion: why it is hard to sustain

Temperature45 million K
Separation: – Relative speed: –
Both nuclei are positive, so they repel. Only very fast (very hot) nuclei get close enough for the strong force to join them. Reaching and holding these temperatures is why fusion is hard to sustain.
Temperature scale is a simplified model: the real D–T temperature is over 100 million K.

Binding energy per nucleon curve

Heavy nuclei sit on the right-hand slope: adding nucleons lowers the binding energy per nucleon slightly. Splitting a heavy nucleus gives products higher up the curve, and that increase is released as energy. Data are real measured values (rounded).

Fusion: mass lost and binding energy gained

Bars show the average binding energy per nucleon of all the nucleons taking part, before and after. The nuclei must be very hot (over 108 K) to get close enough to fuse.

Where fusion happens on the curve

Light nuclei sit on the steep left-hand side of the curve. Fusing them moves the nucleons a long way up, so a lot of energy is released for each kilogram of fuel.
